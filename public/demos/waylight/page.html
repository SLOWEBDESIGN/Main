Why long pages lose people

Most readers don't quit because the writing is bad. They quit because their eyes have nowhere to land.

Most people do not read a web page from top to bottom. They skim the first lines, jump to whatever looks bold, and leave the moment the words start to blur together. It is not a lack of interest. Long lines of evenly weighted text give the eye nothing to hold on to.

Print solved some of this with margins, columns, and careful typesetting. Screens undid a lot of it. A paragraph that looks comfortable on a phone stretches into a gray slab on a laptop, and the same reader who finished a novel last week loses their place three sentences in.

There are two simple ways to help. The first is to give each word an anchor, a slightly heavier start the eye can catch as it moves along the line. The second is to show one line at a time, dimming the rest of the page so there is only one place to look.

Neither changes the words. Nothing is rewritten, summarized, or simplified. The page stays exactly what its author made; only the way you see it shifts.

That matters for anyone who reads for work, studies late, or finds that their attention drifts more on a screen than it ever did on paper. It matters for the people who build websites too, because every reader who loses their place is a reader who stops reading.

Try it on this page. Turn the anchors off and on, switch on line focus and click a line, or change the reading font. Everything you see is the real Waylight extension, running here the same way it runs in your browser.
